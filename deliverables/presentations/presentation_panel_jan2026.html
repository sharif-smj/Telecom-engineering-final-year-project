SLIDE 3 THE PROBLEM

Rural Uganda's Connectivity Challenge

7.5M Ugandans without mobile access
117 Sub-counties below −90 dBm signal
34% Blocked call rate in worst areas (Jinja)

Why GSM Still Matters

While cities enjoy 4G/5G, rural Uganda depends on 2G/GSM for voice calls and Mobile Money (MoMo). Feature phones dominate these areas, and GSM's 900 MHz frequency provides better coverage than higher-frequency technologies. There is no regulatory roadmap for 2G shutdown in Uganda.

"Over 70% of call failures in affected areas were traced to interference, not coverage." — UCC Quality of Service Audit, 2019
SLIDE 4 UNDERSTANDING NOISE

Three Types of Noise Affecting GSM in Uganda

📡

1. Illegal Signal Boosters

Cheap, unregulated amplifiers purchased by residents enter oscillation states, broadcasting wideband noise across GSM frequencies. They act as high-power jammers.

Effect: "Near-far" problem desensitizes base stations

📻

2. "Bizindaalo" Broadcasters

Unlicensed community FM radios use transmitters with poor harmonic filtering. A station at 100 MHz generates harmonics directly in the 900 MHz GSM band.

Effect: Raises thermal noise floor

🌧️

3. Tropical Rain Fade

Uganda's equatorial location brings rainstorms exceeding 100mm/h. This causes severe atmospheric attenuation affecting both the radio path and backhaul links.

Effect: Time-varying signal degradation

Why This Matters

These three noise sources are documented by UCC and are specific to Uganda's environment. Any solution must address these particular interference patterns, not just generic "white noise."

SLIDE 5 CURRENT SOLUTIONS

How Is Noise Currently Mitigated?

Current Approach How It Works Limitation for Rural GSM
UCC Enforcement Confiscate illegal boosters, shut down Bizindaalo Reactive, not preventive; porous borders allow re-entry
Massive MIMO / Beamforming Spatially filter interference using antenna arrays Only available for 4G/5G, not legacy 2G infrastructure
NBI Fiber Expansion Replace microwave backhaul with fiber Addresses backhaul, not the radio access network
Adaptive Power Control Reduce overall noise pollution Does not help classify weak signals in high interference
Traditional Classifiers Likelihood tests, cumulant extractors Accuracy drops below 72% when SNR falls below 0 dB ✗
The Gap: No solution currently cleans up the GSM signal at the receiver before classification
SLIDE 6 OUR SOLUTION

What We Propose: A Two-Stage Approach

Instead of trying to classify noisy signals directly, we first clean the signal, then classify it.

Our DAE–AMC Pipeline (Simplified)

Noisy GSM Signal
(Corrupted by boosters, Bizindaalo, rain)
→
Stage 1: DENOISER
Learns to remove interference
→
Clean Signal
(Noise reduced)
→
Stage 2: CLASSIFIER
Identifies modulation type
→
GMSK / GFSK / QPSK
(Accurate result)

Stage 1: Denoising Autoencoder (DAE)

A neural network that learns what "clean" GSM signals look like. When given a noisy signal, it reconstructs the clean version by removing the interference patterns it has learned to recognize.

Stage 2: Automatic Modulation Classifier (AMC)

A neural network that identifies the modulation type (GMSK, GFSK, QPSK). It works much better on clean signals than noisy ones.

SLIDE 7 NOVELTY

What Makes Our Work New?

🌍

First Application to Uganda's Noise Profile

Previous DAE-AMC research uses generic noise. We train on the specific interference documented by UCC: booster oscillation, Bizindaalo harmonics, and tropical rain fade.

📱

GSM/GMSK Focus

Prior work targets diverse modulations (BPSK, 16QAM, 64QAM). We focus specifically on GMSK—the actual modulation used by GSM networks in rural Uganda.

📊

UCUSAF Edge-Case Design

We specifically target the −90 dBm signal strength floor where UCUSAF operates—not generic "low SNR" conditions.

⚡

Lightweight for Rural Deployment

Our models use fewer than 500K parameters, making them suitable for resource-constrained base stations in rural areas.

SLIDE 9 METHODOLOGY

Our Research Methodology

Step 1: Data Preparation

  • Download RadioML 2018.01A (synthetic, labeled)
  • Download RF Signal Data from Kaggle (real captures)
  • Extract GSM-family modulations only
  • Split into training (70%), validation (15%), test (15%)

Step 2: Uganda-Specific Noise Injection

  • Booster oscillation: Wideband impulse noise
  • Bizindaalo harmonics: Narrowband tones at 900 MHz
  • Rain fade: Time-varying attenuation model

Step 3: Model Training

  • Train DAE on noisy/clean signal pairs
  • Train baseline AMC on raw signals
  • Create combined DAE→AMC pipeline

Step 4: Evaluation

  • Compare accuracy at different noise levels (SNR)
  • Test on −90 dBm to −60 dBm range (UCUSAF conditions)
  • Measure improvement: How much better is DAE+AMC vs AMC alone?

Step 5: Demonstration

  • Build simple visualization interface
  • Show before/after denoising
  • Simulate UCC monitoring workflow

SLIDE 12 CONCLUSION

Summary

The Problem

Rural Uganda depends on GSM networks that suffer from severe interference—illegal boosters, Bizindaalo broadcasters, and tropical rain fade—causing call failures and blocking network expansion in 117 underserved sub-counties.

Our Solution

A two-stage machine learning pipeline: a Denoising Autoencoder (DAE) to clean signals corrupted by Uganda-specific interference, followed by an Automatic Modulation Classifier (AMC) to accurately identify modulation types even at low signal strength.

Our Contribution

The first documented application of DAE-based signal enhancement to Uganda's unique GSM interference environment, with models designed for practical deployment in UCC spectrum monitoring workflows.

We have a clear problem, a focused scope, a novel approach, and a practical application.
